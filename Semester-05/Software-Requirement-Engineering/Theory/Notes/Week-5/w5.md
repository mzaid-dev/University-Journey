# **1. Analysis of Existing Systems**

**Definition (exam-ready):**

Analysis of existing systems is the process of studying current manual systems, old software, or existing workflows to understand how an organization works and what the new software should achieve.

**Simple explanation:**

Before developing new software, we first study the current system.

We need to understand:

- How does the current system work?
- What problems do users face?
- What improvements are needed?
- What should the new system provide?

**Example: University Management System**

Imagine your university manages attendance manually.

The requirements engineer studies:

1. How teachers record attendance.
2. How students check attendance.
3. How administration manages records.
4. What problems exist in the current process.

After studying everything, the engineer identifies requirements for a new attendance management system.

## **Six things examined during existing system analysis**

Your teacher explains these on page 5.

| Item               | Simple meaning                          |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| User Documents     | Manuals and user guides                 |
| Requirements Docs  | Existing system requirements            |
| Change Histories   | Records of previous changes             |
| Discourse Analysis | Study of words and communication        |
| Internal Memos     | Internal messages and policy notes      |
| Development Docs   | Design, architecture and code documents |

Let's understand the difficult ones:

- **Change Histories:** Show what was changed in the old system and when.
- **Discourse Analysis:** Studies words and phrases used by people to understand their communication.
- **Internal Memos:** Help understand internal decisions and policies.
- **Development Docs:** Explain the technical design and working of existing software.

Exam question

**Q: Why is analysis of existing systems important?**

Answer:

It helps requirements engineers understand current workflows, identify existing problems, and find the requirements needed for the new system.

**Remember:** Study the existing system before designing a new one.

**Definition (exam-ready):**

Ethnography is a qualitative research method in which researchers observe and participate in people's daily activities to understand their behavior, culture, and social interactions.

**Simple explanation:**

In ethnography, the requirements engineer goes to the actual working environment and observes how people perform their daily tasks.

Instead of only asking questions, the engineer watches what users actually do.

**Example:**

Suppose a university wants a new library management system.

The requirements engineer:

- Visits the university library.
- Observes how students borrow books.
- Watches how librarians return books.
- Records problems in the existing process.
- Identifies hidden user needs.

This is ethnography.

## **Five important characteristics**

1. **Observational:** Watches real user activities.
2. **Context-driven:** Studies users in their natural environment.
3. **Discover hidden needs:** Finds problems users may not explain.
4. **Empirical and practical:** Uses evidence from actual work.
5. **Generates rich insights:** Helps create better user-centered requirements.

## **Ethnography process**

Your page 7 diagram explains five steps:

1. **Plan the Study**

   Define goals, users and observation focus.
2. **Observe**

   Watch and record users in their real environment.
3. **Document**

   Record activities, interactions and problems.
4. **Analyze**

   Identify patterns, issues and improvement opportunities.
5. **Derive Requirements**

   Convert findings into clear requirements.

**Exam-ready answer: Advantages of Ethnography**

- Helps discover hidden requirements.
- Shows actual user behavior.
- Provides real-world information.
- Improves user-centered system design.

**Important difference:**

- Interview = Ask users what they do.
- Ethnography = Observe what users actually do.

**Definition (exam-ready):**

An interview is a requirements elicitation technique in which a requirements engineer communicates directly with stakeholders to collect information about their needs, problems, and expectations.

**Simple explanation:**

The requirements engineer asks questions from stakeholders to understand what they need from the software.

For example, a developer interviews university students to understand what features they need in a student portal.

## **Types of Interviews**

Your PDF explains two main types on page 10.

**1. Closed Interview**

A closed interview uses a predefined set of questions.

Example:

- Do you use the university portal?
- Yes or No?
- Do you want assignment notifications?
- Yes or No?

**2. Open Interview**

An open interview has no fixed agenda. The requirements engineer allows stakeholders to explain their needs freely.

Example:

"What problems do you face while using the university portal?"

The student can explain everything in their own words.

## **Difference between Open and Closed Interviews**

| Closed Interview | Open Interview     |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| Fixed questions  | No fixed questions |
| Limited answers  | Detailed answers   |
| Easy to compare  | More flexible      |
| Less exploration | More exploration   |

## **Interview Session Steps**

Very important

Your teacher divides the interview process into three steps.

**1. Opening**

- Build trust and credibility.
- Introduce yourself.
- Explain the interview purpose.
- Explain the time and objectives.

**2. Body**

- Ask questions.
- Listen carefully.
- Take accurate notes.
- Ask follow-up questions.

**3. Closing**

- Summarize the findings.
- Answer questions.
- Agree on next steps.
- Arrange another meeting if needed.
- Thank the interviewee.

### **Important interview questions**

Your slides divide questions into different categories.

**A. Start-up questions**

These help understand the overall problem.

Examples:

- Who will use the system?
- What problem are we solving?
- When do you need the system?
- What is your budget?
- What resources are available?

**B. Functional requirement questions**

These identify what the system must do.

Examples:

- What functions should the system provide?
- What inputs should it accept?
- What outputs should it produce?

**C. Design constraint questions**

These identify system limitations.

Three examples from your slides:

- Physical environment: Where will the equipment be located?
- Interfaces: Will the system connect with other systems?
- Standards: Are there any required standards?

**D. Performance questions**

- What should the response time be?
- How fast should the system work?
- What are the execution speed requirements?

**E. Precision and Accuracy questions**

- How accurate should calculations be?
- What level of precision is required?

**F. Usability and Human Factors**

- How easy should the system be to use?
- What training will users need?

**G. Security questions**

- Should access be controlled?
- Should users' data be separated?
- What security precautions are needed?

## **Important concept: Problem-oriented questions**

Your teacher gives an important example on page 8.

Less effective

"Would you like Word 2007, Excel 2007 or both?"

Better question

"Would you like to do word processing, computations, or both?"

**Why is the second question better?**

Because it focuses on the actual user needs instead of suggesting a specific software solution.

**Exam tip:** A requirements engineer should understand the problem before deciding on the solution.

# **4. Brainstorming**

[thisisanitsupportgroup.com](https://thisisanitsupportgroup.com/blog/system-design-interview-complete-guide-software-engineers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Definition (exam-ready):**

Brainstorming is a group or individual creativity technique used to generate a large number of ideas to solve a problem or identify requirements.

**Simple explanation:**

Different people share their ideas freely. The team collects these ideas and selects the best ones.

**Example:**

A university wants to improve its student portal.

The team gathers students, teachers and developers.

They suggest:

- Online attendance.
- Assignment reminders.
- Quiz results.
- GPA tracking.
- Course registration.

The team then reviews and selects the most useful ideas.

## **Two main activities of Brainstorming**

Stage 1

**The Storm – Generate ideas**

- Generate as many ideas as possible.
- Quantity is more important than quality.
- Do not criticize ideas.
- Encourage creative and unusual ideas.
- Record every idea.

Stage 2

**The Calm – Evaluate ideas**

- Review all ideas.
- Remove unsuitable ideas.
- Combine similar ideas.
- Improve and clarify ideas.
- Rank ideas by priority.
- Select the best ideas.

**Easy way to remember:**

Storm = Generate.

Calm = Filter and select.

## **Four Core Principles**

1. **Defer Judgment:** Do not criticize ideas during generation.
2. **Go for Quantity:** Generate as many ideas as possible.
3. **Encourage Wild Ideas:** Allow unusual and creative ideas.
4. **Build on Others:** Improve or combine other people's ideas.

## **Difference between Storm and Calm**

| Storm            | Calm            |
| ---------------- | --------------- |
| Generate ideas   | Evaluate ideas  |
| Quantity matters | Quality matters |
| No criticism     | Review ideas    |
| Free thinking    | Prioritization  |

**Exam-ready question: Why is brainstorming useful?**

Brainstorming is useful because it generates many ideas, encourages creativity, involves different stakeholders, and helps identify the best requirements.

High-priority topic

**Definition (exam-ready):**

Prototyping is the process of creating an early and simplified model of a product or system to test ideas, understand requirements, and collect feedback before full development.

**Simple explanation:**

Before building the complete software, developers create a sample version to show users how the system may look or work.

Users review it and explain what they like or what needs improvement.

**Example:**

Suppose we are developing a university portal.

Before developing the complete portal, we create sample screens for:

- Login.
- Dashboard.
- Attendance.
- Assignments.
- Results.

Students review these screens and give feedback.

Developers use this feedback to improve the requirements.

## **Why is Prototyping used?**

According to page 24:

1. Helps developers and users understand requirements.
2. Clarifies incomplete requirements.
3. Helps identify new functionalities.
4. Improves usability.
5. Helps establish priorities.
6. Resolves uncertainties early.
7. Encourages user participation.

## **Types of Prototyping**

Must learn all four

Your teacher explains four types on page 25.

### **1. Horizontal Prototype**

Focuses on one layer of the system.

**Example:** A complete clickable UI of a mobile application with screens and buttons, but without a real database or backend logic.

Simple meaning: Shows many screens but not the complete internal working.

### **2. Vertical Prototype**

Focuses on one complete slice of the system.

**Example:** A fully working login feature connected to a real database, while the other application features are not developed.

Simple meaning: One feature works completely from front to back.

### **3. Evolutive Prototype**

A prototype that is continuously improved and developed into the final product.

**Example:** An MVP of a ride-sharing application that starts with basic features and gradually gains more features.

Simple meaning: Build, improve, and keep developing.

### **4. Throw-away Prototype**

A temporary prototype created to understand or validate requirements. It is discarded after feedback is collected.

**Example:** A rough paper sketch of a mobile application used to decide between two screen layouts.

Simple meaning: Build, learn, and discard.

## **Difference between the four types**

| Type       | Main focus                 |
| ---------- | -------------------------- |
| Horizontal | One layer                  |
| Vertical   | One complete slice         |
| Evolutive  | Improve into final product |
| Throw-away | Temporary; discard later   |

**Very important:** Do not confuse Horizontal and Vertical.

- Horizontal = Breadth across one layer.
- Vertical = Depth through one complete feature.

## **Prototyping – Fidelity**

Fidelity means how closely a prototype looks and behaves like the final product.

Your page 26 explains:

**1. Low-Fidelity Prototype**

A simple and basic model.

Example: Paper sketches and simple wireframes.

**2. High-Fidelity Prototype**

A detailed and interactive model that looks and behaves much like the final product.

Example: A clickable and detailed mobile application design.

**3. Digital and Physical Prototypes**

- Digital: Virtual software mockups.
- Physical: Tangible models made using materials such as foam or 3D prints.

## **Risks of Prototyping**

Important for long questions and MCQs

Your page 27 explains five risks.

| Risk                  | Simple explanation                                                               |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Almost Done Fallacy   | Users think the complete software is ready because the prototype looks finished. |
| Scope Creep           | Users keep requesting more features.                                             |
| Evolutive Trap        | A poor throw-away prototype is reused in the final product.                      |
| Wasted Time and Money | Too much time is spent changing small design details.                            |
| Developer Attachment  | Developers become attached to their prototype and resist changes.                |

**Remember:** A prototype is not necessarily a complete working software product.

# **6. Joint Application Development (JAD)**

[All the Prevalent Software Development Methodologies](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/vzSYFGsfZnzA0AtZSEchRqo4dg46hxXjRo2TrSeTJUFBetI2Hxb1dPGh7FGGhO2F7Wv1Ve8y-NrnY_-1KUXLSas2m93GP7zsBVspie8LscbgsUVXMaV5X-aV6HeYHFd-nBd4zgEe0TjcRQCGmPqVNwM5ez7IwIOL9dkOcA44lrs?purpose=inline)

Important topic

**Definition (exam-ready):**

Joint Application Development (JAD) is a structured and collaborative requirements elicitation technique in which stakeholders, end-users, and IT experts work together in facilitated workshops to define and agree on system requirements.

**Simple explanation:**

Instead of collecting requirements separately from different people, JAD brings everyone into one organized meeting.

They discuss problems, share ideas, and agree on requirements.

**Example:**

A university wants to develop a new student portal.

A JAD session includes:

- University administration.
- Students.
- Teachers.
- Developers.
- Requirements analysts.

Everyone discusses the required features and agrees on the final requirements.

## **Four Phases of JAD**

Your page 29 gives four phases.

1. **Define Objectives**

   Identify the main problem, goals, scope and participants.
2. **Session Preparation**

   Prepare the agenda, documents and tools for the workshop.
3. **Session Conduct**

   Conduct the workshop, discuss workflows and agree on requirements.
4. **Documentation and Sign-off**

   Prepare the SRS document, review it and get approval from the executive sponsor.

## **Core Roles in a JAD Session**

Learn these six roles

| Role                 | Responsibility                                         |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Executive Sponsor    | Provides funding and approves final decisions.         |
| Facilitator          | Leads the session and manages discussions.             |
| Users / Stakeholders | Explain their needs and business rules.                |
| IT Representatives   | Explain technical possibilities and limitations.       |
| Scribe               | Records decisions and requirements.                    |
| Observers            | Watch the session without actively speaking or voting. |

### **Understand the roles with an example**

Imagine a university JAD meeting:

- Executive Sponsor: University project owner.
- Facilitator: Person managing the meeting.
- Users: Students and teachers.
- IT Representatives: Developers and analysts.
- Scribe: Person writing meeting notes.
- Observers: People watching the meeting.

**Exam question: What is the responsibility of a facilitator?**

Answer: A facilitator guides the discussion, manages time, resolves conflicts, and ensures that everyone participates.

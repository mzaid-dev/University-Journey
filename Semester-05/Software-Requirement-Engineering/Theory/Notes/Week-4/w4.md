## **1. Requirements Engineering (RE)**

**Definition:**

Requirements Engineering is the systematic process of defining, documenting, and maintaining the needs and constraints of a software system.

In simple words:

It means understanding what the customer needs and documenting those needs before developing software.

**Example:**

Suppose we want to develop a University Management System.

We need to understand:

- What students want.
- What teachers want.
- What administrators want.
- What features the system needs.
- What limitations the system has.

This whole process is part of Requirements Engineering.

**Why is it important?**

- Helps understand customer needs.
- Reduces misunderstandings.
- Prevents unnecessary development.
- Helps build the correct software.

Source: Week 4, page 8.

## **2. Requirements Engineering Process**

Your teacher divides this topic into two major parts:

**A. Requirements Inception**

Understand the project and its basic needs.

**B. Requirements Development**

Discover, analyze, specify and verify requirements.

**Requirements Management**

Continuously document, track, prioritize and control changes.

### **A. Requirements Inception**

**Definition:**

Requirements Inception is the initial activity in which we establish the basic project scope, identify stakeholders, and understand the main business problem.

In simple words: Before starting development, we understand what we are building and why.

**Seven important activities:**

1. Identify Stakeholders – Find people who use, develop, fund, or are affected by the system.
2. Define Business Needs – Identify the main business problem.
3. Establish Product Vision – Define what the product should achieve.
4. Set System Scope – Define what is included and excluded.
5. Preliminary Feasibility Analysis – Check whether the project is technically, financially, and operationally possible.
6. Outline Business Case and Roadmap – Prepare an initial plan and timeline.
7. Produce Initial Artifacts – Prepare a Vision and Scope Document.

**Example: University Management System**

- Stakeholders: Students, teachers, administrators.
- Business need: Reduce manual university work.
- Product vision: Provide an online university platform.
- Scope: Attendance, results, courses.
- Feasibility: Check available budget and technology.

Important for exam

Remember: Inception focuses on the project's initial understanding, not detailed functional requirements.

### **B. Requirements Development**

**Definition:**

Requirements Development is the systematic process of discovering, analyzing, specifying, and validating what a product or system must do before actual design and construction begin.

It has four main activities:

1. Elicitation
2. Analysis
3. Specification
4. Verification

Let's understand each one.

## **3. Four Activities of Requirements Development**

### **1. Requirements Elicitation**

**Definition:**

Requirements Elicitation is the process of discovering requirements by communicating with customers, system users, and other stakeholders.

In simple words: Collecting information about what users need.

**Sources of requirements:**

- Customers and stakeholders.
- Existing systems.
- Documentation.
- Domain experts.

**Requirement gathering techniques:**

| Technique           | Simple meaning                                 |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Brainstorming       | Discuss ideas in a group.                      |
| Interviews          | Ask stakeholders questions.                    |
| Task Observation    | Watch users doing their work.                  |
| Use Cases/Scenarios | Understand how users interact with the system. |
| Prototyping         | Create an early model of the system.           |

**Example:**

You are developing a university portal.

You interview students and ask:

- How do you check your results?
- How do you submit assignments?
- What problems do you face?

Their answers help you collect requirements.

### **2. Requirements Analysis**

**Definition:**

Requirements Analysis is the process of studying stakeholder needs to understand the real problem and develop a suitable solution.

In simple words: Checking collected requirements and making them clear, correct, and practical.

**Main objectives:**

1. Detect and resolve conflicts between requirements.
2. Identify system boundaries.
3. Understand how the system interacts with its environment.
4. Convert system requirements into software requirements.

**Example:**

- Students want easy access to results.
- Teachers want secure control over marks.

During analysis, we identify both needs and resolve possible conflicts.

### **3. Requirements Specification**

**Definition:**

A Requirements Specification is a formal, documented collection of what a system, product, or service must do.

In simple words: Writing all agreed requirements in a proper document.

**Important difference: What vs How**

| Requirements Specification         | Design Specification                       |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Explains WHAT the system must do.  | Explains HOW the system will be built.     |
| Defines features and requirements. | Defines architecture and technical design. |

**Example:**

- What: The system shall allow students to log in.
- How: Developers will implement login using a particular authentication technology.

Remember this difference because it is easy to test in MCQs.

### **4. Requirements Verification**

**Definition:**

Requirements Verification is the process of checking whether documented requirements are clear, complete, consistent, and well-formed.

The main question is:

**"Are we building the product right?"**

Five important goals:

| Goal         | Meaning                                             |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness | Nothing important is missing.                       |
| Consistency  | Requirements do not contradict each other.          |
| Clarity      | Requirements have only one clear meaning.           |
| Testability  | Requirements can be checked with pass/fail results. |
| Feasibility  | Requirements can realistically be developed.        |

**Example:**

Requirement: The application should be fast.

This is unclear because nobody knows how fast.

Better requirement: The application shall load its home page within 2 seconds.

Now it is measurable and testable.

Must remember

**Elicitation = Collect**

**Analysis = Examine**

**Specification = Document**

**Verification = Check**

Source: Week 4, pages 9–16.

## **4. Requirements Management**

**Definition:**

Requirements Management is the continuous process of documenting, analyzing, tracing, prioritizing, and agreeing on project needs while controlling changes throughout the product lifecycle.

In simple words:

It means managing requirements from the beginning until the project is completed.

**Main activities:**

- Document requirements.
- Analyze requirements.
- Track requirements.
- Prioritize requirements.
- Control requirement changes.

### **Important documents in Requirements Management**

Your slide (page 16) mentions:

1. Vision and Scope Document.
2. Elicitation Notes – Raw requirements collected from stakeholders.
3. Problem Domain Requirements Document.
4. System Requirements Document.
5. Software Requirements Document.

**User Requirements vs Detailed Requirements**

| User Requirements              | Detailed Requirements              |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Written for customers.         | Written mainly for developers.     |
| High-level description.        | Detailed description.              |
| Natural language and diagrams. | Structured technical document.     |
| Explain what users need.       | Explain system services in detail. |

**Exam example:**

A student says, "I want to view my semester results."

- User requirement: Student can view semester results.
- Detailed requirement: The system shall display the logged-in student's semester results with course names, marks, and grades.

## **5. Requirements Analysis – Five Steps of Problem Analysis**

High priority

This is one of the most important topics in your Week 4 slides.

Before developing a solution, we must understand the actual problem.

Your teacher explains five steps:

1. **Problem Agreement**

   All stakeholders agree on the exact problem.
2. **Root Cause Analysis**

   Find the real reason behind the problem.
3. **Stakeholder Identification**

   Identify all people affected by or involved in the problem.
4. **Solution Boundary**

   Define what is included and excluded.
5. **Constraints and Success Criteria**

   Identify limitations and how success will be measured.

Let's understand them with one example.

**Scenario: University Portal**

The university portal becomes slow during examination results.

**Step 1: Problem Agreement**

Students, teachers, and management agree that the portal becomes slow during result announcements.

**Step 2: Root Cause Analysis**

Find the actual reason.

Example: Too many students access the portal simultaneously.

**Step 3: Stakeholder Identification**

Identify:

- Students.
- Teachers.
- IT department.
- University management.

**Step 4: Solution Boundary**

Define the project scope.

- In scope: Improve result checking.
- Out of scope: Redesign the complete university website.

**Step 5: Constraints and Success Criteria**

Constraints:

- Limited budget.
- Limited development time.
- Existing server limitations.

Success criteria:

\- Results should load within 2 seconds under the agreed test conditions.

### **Difference between Constraints and Success Criteria**

| Constraints                  | Success Criteria                |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Limitations or restrictions. | Measures of successful results. |
| Fixed budget.                | Reduce processing time by 40%.  |
| Complete within 3 months.    | Achieve 99.9% uptime.           |

**Why are these five steps important?**

- Prevent solving the wrong problem.
- Reduce unnecessary work.
- Control project scope.
- Improve planning.
- Help stakeholders agree.

Source: Week 4, pages 19–22.

## **6. Vision and Scope Document**

**Definition:**

A Vision and Scope Document is a document that defines what the product is, why it is needed, and what it will deliver.

In simple words:

It explains the purpose, goals, and boundaries of a project.

### **Purpose**

1. Communicates product vision.
2. Defines project boundaries.
3. Prevents scope creep.
4. Aligns stakeholders.
5. Supports project planning and validation.

### **Main Sections**

| Section               | What it contains                   |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Business Opportunity  | Why the project is needed.         |
| Problem Statement     | What problem must be solved.       |
| Product Vision        | What the product should achieve.   |
| Goals                 | Expected results.                  |
| Stakeholders          | People involved.                   |
| In-Scope              | Features included.                 |
| Out-of-Scope          | Features excluded.                 |
| Risks and Constraints | Possible problems and limitations. |

**Example: Study Management System**

- Vision: Help students manage their studies.
- Goal: Improve study planning.
- In-scope: Tasks, deadlines, study plans.
- Out-of-scope: Online university fee payments.
- Stakeholders: Students and teachers.

### **What is Scope Creep?**

Scope creep happens when project requirements increase beyond the original agreed scope without proper adjustments to time, budget, or resources.

**Example:**

Initially, a university requests a student attendance system.

During development, they keep adding:

- Online fee payment.
- Hostel management.
- Transport management.

These additional features increase the project scope.

That is scope creep.

## **7. Requirements Modelling**

**Definition:**

Requirements Modelling is the practice of creating structured, visual representations of system requirements using diagrams, flowcharts, and object models.

In simple words:

It means representing requirements visually so everyone can understand the system easily.

### **Goal-Based Modelling**

Goal-Based Modelling connects stakeholder goals with system requirements.

It explains WHY a system is needed before deciding WHAT it should do or HOW it should work.

**Example:**

University Management System:

**Business Goal**

Improve university management.

**User Goals**

Students check results; teachers upload marks.

**System Requirements**

Result management and marks management features.

### **Types of Goal Models**

Your slide on page 26 explains four types.

**1. AND/OR Decomposition**

Breaks a main goal into smaller required or alternative sub-goals.

- AND: All sub-goals must be achieved.
- OR: Any one alternative can achieve the goal.

Example:

Goal: Student accesses university services.

- AND: Login AND view results AND view courses.
- OR: Login using email OR student ID.

**2. Softgoal Model**

Represents quality goals that are difficult to measure exactly.

Examples:

- Usability.
- Security.
- Speed.

**3. Actor Dependency Model**

Shows who depends on whom for goals and resources.

Example:

A student depends on a teacher to publish marks.

**4. Obstacle/Conflict Model**

Identifies risks, barriers, and competing goals.

Example:

Students want quick access to results, while the university requires strict security checks.

### **Construction Steps of Goal Models**

Remember these five steps:

1. Identify stakeholders and their goals.
2. Define measurable success criteria.
3. Break goals into sub-goals, tasks, and constraints.
4. Analyze conflicts, risks, and dependencies.
5. Prioritize goals and derive requirements.

Source: Week 4, pages 24–27.

## **8. Requirements Elicitation – Detailed Understanding**

We already studied its definition. Now let's cover its goals, sources, problems, and tasks.

### **Goals of Elicitation**

Your teacher identifies five main goals:

1. Discover Clear Project Scope – Understand what the system must and must not do.
2. Uncover Unstated Needs – Find requirements that users do not mention directly.
3. Bridge Communication Gaps – Improve communication between developers and stakeholders.
4. Build Consensus and Buy-In – Bring stakeholders to a common agreement.
5. Establish Feasibility – Check whether requirements are achievable.

**Example of an unstated need:**

A student asks for a result-checking feature.

After observing students, you discover they also need notifications when results are published.

The notification feature is an unstated need discovered during elicitation.

### **Elicitation Risks and Challenges**

These are the main problems mentioned in pages 30–31.

| Problem                   | Explanation                                         |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Problems of Scope         | System boundaries are unclear or defined too early. |
| Problems of Understanding | Stakeholders cannot clearly explain their needs.    |
| Conflicting Requirements  | Different stakeholders want different things.       |
| Problems of Volatility    | Requirements keep changing.                         |
| Limited Availability      | Experts are not easily available.                   |
| Poor Vocabulary           | Stakeholders and developers use different terms.    |
| Resistance to Change      | People do not want to adopt new systems.            |

**Important concept:**

Good technology and tools cannot replace proper communication with stakeholders.

### **Sources of Requirements**

According to page 32:

1. Clients and customers.
2. Current and future users.
3. Managers and domain experts.
4. Developers and QA teams.
5. Pre-existing systems.
6. Existing documentation.
7. Competing systems.
8. Standards, policies, and legislation.
9. Documentation about interfacing systems.
10. The person who pays for development.

### **Stakeholder Availability**

Stakeholders are generally busy because:

- They have other priorities.
- They have daily work responsibilities.
- They consider elicitation an additional task.
- They may fear losing their jobs, power, or recognition because of a new system.

**How can we handle this?**

- Get management support.
- Schedule meetings properly.
- Respect stakeholder responsibilities.
- Explain the benefits of the new system.
- Make stakeholders feel involved.

## **9. Tasks of Elicitation**

Your last two slides explain four major tasks.

1. **Prepare for Elicitation**
   - Define scope.
   - Select suitable techniques.
   - Identify and schedule stakeholders.
   - Prepare questions and meeting agendas.
2. **Conduct Elicitation**
   - Explore stakeholder needs.
   - Focus on WHY rather than immediately discussing technical solutions.
   - Capture raw information.
3. **Confirm Elicitation Results**
   - Review collected information.
   - Find missing or conflicting requirements.
   - Resolve conflicts through discussion.
   - Confirm accuracy with stakeholders.
4. **Document and Package the Output**
   - Categorize requirements.
   - Prepare user stories.
   - Create use cases.
   - Prepare a prioritized product backlog.

**Easy way to remember:**

Prepare → Conduct → Confirm → Document.

Source: Week 4, pages 28–35.

## **10. Agile Requirements Workflow**

Your Week 4 slides (pages 3–5) explain six sequential steps.

Important – Learn the sequence

1. **Kickoff (Vision + Scope)**

   Establish the project vision and high-level scope.
2. **Backlog (Epics + Stories)**

   Create a prioritized list of features, bug fixes, technical work, and improvements.
3. **Sprint Planning (Select + Commit)**

   Select backlog items and commit to the sprint goal.
4. **Build and Test (Increment)**

   Develop and test selected requirements to create a working product increment.
5. **Review (Feedback)**

   Demonstrate the product increment to stakeholders and collect feedback.
6. **Retrospective (Improve Process)**

   Review the team's performance and improve the working process.

**Example: StudySync AI**

- Kickoff: Decide to build a student study planner.
- Backlog: Add login, tasks, deadlines, and study plans.
- Sprint Planning: Select login and task management.
- Build and Test: Develop and test these features.
- Review: Show the features to stakeholders.
- Retrospective: Discuss what went well and what needs improvement.

### **SRS vs Product Backlog**

This is another important comparison.

| SRS                                                  | Product Backlog                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mostly static document.                              | Dynamic and changing list.                                 |
| Requirements documented upfront.                     | Requirements updated continuously.                         |
| Contains functional and non-functional requirements. | Contains features, bugs, technical work, and enhancements. |
| Used in traditional development.                     | Commonly used in Agile development.                        |

**Remember:**

SRS = Documented requirements.

Product Backlog = Continuously updated work list.

Source: Week 4, pages 3–5.

# **11. How to solve scenario-based exam questions**

Bro, understanding definitions is not enough. You must also identify concepts from real situations.

Use this method:

| If the question says...                 | Your answer                 |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Collecting user needs                   | Elicitation                 |
| Finding the real problem                | Analysis                    |
| Writing requirements                    | Specification               |
| Checking clarity and completeness       | Verification                |
| Controlling requirement changes         | Requirements Management     |
| Defining project vision and boundaries  | Inception                   |
| Finding the root reason                 | Root Cause Analysis         |
| Defining included and excluded features | Solution Boundary           |
| Measuring project success               | Success Criteria            |
| Showing goals visually                  | Goal-Based Modelling        |
| Preparing before stakeholder meetings   | Prepare for Elicitation     |
| Reviewing collected information         | Confirm Elicitation Results |

### **Practice scenarios**

**Scenario 1:**

A developer interviews students to understand their requirements for an online examination system.

What activity is being performed?

Answer: Requirements Elicitation.

**Scenario 2:**

Students want quick result access, but administrators want strict security.

Which activity helps resolve this conflict?

Answer: Requirements Analysis.

**Scenario 3:**

A requirement says, "The system should be user-friendly." The team changes it to a measurable requirement.

Which activity is performed?

Answer: Requirements Verification.

**Scenario 4:**

The university decides that hostel management will not be included in the first release.

Which concept is this?

Answer: Solution Boundary (Out-of-Scope).

**Scenario 5:**

The team maintains a list of features, bugs, and improvements and updates it after stakeholder feedback.

Answer: Product Backlog.

**Scenario 6:**

The team discusses what went wrong during the previous sprint and how to improve.

Answer: Retrospective.
